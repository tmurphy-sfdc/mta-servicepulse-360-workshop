Lab 1: Extend the Case Object for Multi-Agency Intake

ServicePulse 360 Workshop v0.7 (revision 12) | Estimated time: 104 minutes | You will create the ServicePulse 360 Lightning App, customize the standard Salesforce Case object (Priority field, Type field) for MTA's multi-agency operations, add two custom fields where standard genuinely can't fit, place those fields on the Lightning record page via Dynamic Forms, and finish by creating a Golden Record you'll use throughout the rest of the workshop.

What you're building and why

MTA's production Salesforce environment (Cassie) runs on the standard Case object. So does almost every Service Cloud implementation. This lab teaches the most common pattern in real-world Salesforce administration: taking standard objects and standard fields and configuring them to match a specific business context. You won't build a parallel custom object. You won't even add custom picklists for things the standard fields can already model. You'll customize the standard picklists' values.

By the end of this lab, your org will have a dedicated ServicePulse 360 Lightning App, four agency-specific Record Types on the standard Case object (NYCT, LIRR, MNR, B&T), the standard Priority and Type picklists customized to MTA's vocabulary (P1 through P4 for Priority, 13 transit-specific values for Type), two custom fields where standard genuinely doesn't fit, a tailored page layout, both custom fields placed on the Lightning record page's canvas via Dynamic Forms, and a Golden Record (a well-populated Case) that you'll use as the central example throughout the rest of the two-day workshop.

Why this mattersThe principle is customize before extending. A custom field is justified only when configuration of the standard field genuinely can't get you there. Not when the default values are wrong. Not when the default label is wrong. In this lab you'll see the test applied four times: twice it produces "customize the standard," twice it produces "add a custom field."

What is "Pronto" and why does my org have restaurant data?

Your workshop org is built on Salesforce's "Pronto" reference environment, a fictitious food-delivery business that uses Salesforce to manage its restaurant partners, menus, deliveries, and customer support. Salesforce ships this org as a hands-on training environment because it has realistic data, configured agents, and pre-built automation. Your job over the next two days isn't to build for Pronto. Your job is to customize the same standard Salesforce platform that runs Pronto's operations to instead match MTA's operations. Wherever you see references to Storefronts, Menus, or Merchant Operations, those are starter content. We'll customize Case to MTA's vocabulary while leaving the starter content alone, the same way Cassie sits on the same Salesforce platform that runs every other Service Cloud in the world.

The decision applied four times

Severity to standard Priority. Standard Priority's values are High/Medium/Low; we need P1 through P4. Wrong values, right field. Customize the picklist values, keep the field standard.
Issue Type to standard Type. Standard Type's values in this org are restaurant-themed. We need transit-specific values. Wrong values, right field. Customize the picklist values.
Resolution Notes to custom field. Standard Case has Description (the original report) but no dedicated resolution-narrative field. No standard field to configure. Add a custom field.
Reported Date/Time to custom field. Standard CreatedDate exists but is read-only and can't be back-dated. Configuration can't make CreatedDate writable. Add a custom field.

What you keep from standard Case unchanged

Subject (short summary line). Standard fits.
Description (full text of the report). Standard fits.
Status (New / On Hold / Escalated / Closed). Standard fits.
Priority (same field, we just change its picklist values).
Type (same field, the new picklist values were pre-deployed; we'll tour those in Part 2.2).
Contact (who reported). Standard fits.
Account (agency or vendor). Standard fits.
Owner, escalation rules, case history, comments: all standard.

It does NOT control the field arrangement on the Lightning record page itself. In Part 9 we'll place fields on the Lightning record page via Dynamic Forms (which is a separate system). Right now in Part 6 we're configuring the classic Case Layout for its real jobs.

ServicePulse 360 Workshop v0.7 (revision 4) | Estimated time: 90 minutes | Using the standard Asset object, you will prepare the Asset Layout, model an NYCT bus as a parent Asset with four component Assets (battery pack, HVAC unit, drive motor, farebox), create peer relationships via Asset Relationship, and connect your golden Case record to the bus Asset.

What you're building and why

MTA operates physical infrastructure: buses, trains, bridges, tunnels, signal cabins, station equipment, fare collection systems. Each is an Asset. Each is also made of components that break down, get replaced, and need their own service history. A bus has a battery pack, a drive motor, an HVAC unit, a farebox. Each component is itself an Asset.

The standard Salesforce Asset object (plus its sibling Asset Relationship) models this directly. In this lab you'll prepare the Asset Layout to show the fields and related lists you need, create one parent Asset (an NYCT bus) with four child component Assets built directly from the parent's Child Assets related list, link one component to another via Asset Relationship, and connect your golden Case record from Lab 1 to the parent bus. The Case record page's Other related cases tab (built in Lab 2) will gain a second Related List - Single component showing the Asset's Cases.

Why this mattersAsset is the standard-first way to model physical infrastructure in Salesforce. Teams unfamiliar with Asset often build custom objects (Equipment__c, Vehicle__c, Component__c) to model the same thing. Standard Asset gives you Asset Relationship for parent-child hierarchies, Asset State Period for state history, Maintenance Plan integration for preventive maintenance scheduling (via Work Orders), and a direct link to Case for service history, all without custom-building anything. The Maintenance Plan integration is particularly powerful: define a maintenance cadence on the Asset, and Salesforce auto-generates Work Orders on schedule.

The hierarchy we're building

Two levels, five Assets total (one parent bus, four children):

Level 1 (parent): NYCT Bus 4521, the whole bus.
Level 2 (children): Battery Pack 4521-BP, HVAC Unit 4521-HV, Drive Motor 4521-DM, Farebox 4521-FB.

Each component is a child of the bus via the Parent Asset field (the direct hierarchy you'll build in Parts 4-5). Asset Relationships (Part 6) model peer-to-peer links between Assets at the same level, not the hierarchy itself. The children can have their own Cases (an issue specific to the battery pack vs. the bus overall), and both the bus and each component can appear on the Related List - Single component you built in Lab 2.

Part 6: Use Asset Relationship for peer relationships (8 min)

Parent Asset is a simple one-to-many hierarchy. For richer relationships (replacement history, dependencies, qualified peer links), use AssetRelationship.

Why both patterns?Parent Asset is for ownership-style hierarchy (a battery belongs to a bus). Asset Relationship is for peer or qualified relationships (this motor replaced the previous one, this battery depends on this BMS).

6.1: Create an Asset Relationship between Battery Pack and HVAC Unit

1.Navigate to Battery Pack 4521-BP. Click into its record.
2.Scroll to the Related Assets related list.
3.Click New.
4.Related Asset: pick HVAC Unit 4521-HV.
5.Relationship Type: Replacement.
6.From Date: today.
7.Click Save.
Verified? Great job.On Battery Pack 4521-BP's Related Assets list, you now see one row: HVAC Unit 4521-HV with Relationship Type = Replacement, with its own Asset Relationship Number. This is the standard Asset Relationship object doing the peer link.
How Asset Relationship directionality worksAsset Relationship records appear on the Primary Asset's Related Assets list, in this case, Battery Pack 4521-BP. They do NOT appear on the Related Asset's Related Assets list (HVAC Unit 4521-HV). If you want the relationship to appear on both Assets, you'd create a second Asset Relationship record with Battery Pack as the Related Asset and HVAC Unit as the Primary. For most workshops and most production use cases, one direction is sufficient.

Lab 4 - Field Service Foundations

ServicePulse 360 Workshop v0.7 (revision 4) | Estimated time: 60 minutes | Create a Work Type, trim the Work Order Layout to the fields that matter for MTA, escalate your golden Case record into a Work Order, and verify the Case-Asset-Work Order relationship chain.

What you're building and why

Field Service is Salesforce's standard framework for coordinating work done on physical assets. In Labs 1 through 3 we modeled WHAT the system knows (Cases, Agencies, Assets). Lab 4 introduces HOW work gets scheduled against those Assets. A dispatcher takes a Case about a signal fault on NYCT Bus 4521 and escalates it into a Work Order: a work item routed to a technician, tied to the Asset, with a defined scope of work (Work Type). The chain Case to Work Order to Asset becomes the backbone of maintenance operations.

In this lab you'll create one Work Type (Signal Repair), trim the standard Work Order Layout to only the fields that matter for MTA, escalate your golden Case record from Lab 1 into a Work Order, and verify the Work Order inherits the Case's Asset. The subsequent hands-on labs extend this with Service Appointment (scheduling) and Service Resource (technician assignment).

Why this mattersField Service is a load-bearing standard capability in Salesforce. Teams unfamiliar with Field Service often build custom Work Order custom objects to coordinate work. They lose the Work Order-to-Asset wiring, the Service Appointment scheduling framework, the mobile Field Service app, and the Maintenance Plan integration mentioned in Lab 3. Standard Field Service is the right starting point for any maintenance-oriented use case.

The chain you're building

Your golden Case record (NYCT, Account = MTA New York City Transit, Asset = NYCT Bus 4521) created in Lab 1.
A Work Type you create (Signal Repair), the catalog of 'kinds of work' MTA can dispatch.
A Work Order escalated from the golden Case, the specific work item. Inherits the Case's Asset.
The chain is bidirectional: a dispatcher can view the Case's Work Orders, OR the Asset's Work Orders. Standard wiring, no custom work.

Part 1 - Add a Validation Rule to Service Disruption (10 min)

Validation Rules enforce data integrity at save time. We'll build the Validation Rule that enforces the ordering.

2.Click Validation Rules in the left column.
3.Click New.

1.2 - Build the duration-ordering rule

Navigate: You just clicked New Validation Rule from Setup > Object Manager > Service Disruption > Validation Rules (1.1).

1.Rule Name: Cleared_After_Started.
2.Active: check.
3.Description: Prevents saving a Service Disruption where Disruption Cleared Date/Time is on or before Disruption Start Date/Time. Ensures the operations duration math is always positive.
4.Error Condition Formula: paste the formula below (click Insert Field as needed). The rule fires when this formula evaluates to TRUE.
NOT(ISBLANK(Disruption_Cleared_Date_Time__c)) && NOT(ISBLANK(Disruption_Start_Date_Time__c)) && Disruption_Cleared_Date_Time__c <= Disruption_Start_Date_Time__c

Part 1 - Validation Rule (10 min)

Teaching moment'Validation Rules are the simplest form of business logic enforcement in Salesforce. One formula, applied at save time. No code, no Flows, no Apex. If the formula evaluates to TRUE, the save fails. If FALSE, save succeeds. In MTA's context, we use it to enforce that Disruption Cleared Date/Time is after Disruption Start Date/Time, so Operations can trust the duration math. Dozens of these across every production org.'
Teaching moment'The Duration (Minutes) formula field is where this lab stops being about recordkeeping and starts being about measurement. Average Duration by Agency is the single most important operational metric for MTA's responsiveness. We compute it with a one-line formula and surface it on a Dashboard chart. Four fields, two Reports, one chart. Measurement unlocked.'
Listen forSomeone asking "when should I use a Validation Rule vs a Flow?" Answer: "Validation Rules are the right choice when you want to BLOCK a save based on data conditions. Flows are the right choice when you want to CHANGE data or trigger downstream actions. If the goal is prevention, Validation Rule. If the goal is reaction, Flow."

Each batch below gets one framing moment at the start; once participants are building, let them run with minimal interruption, and roam the room rather than narrating every click.

Real-world context: who's actually calling this in

In the real world, this report wouldn't originate with the dispatcher. It would come down through Rail Traffic Control (RTC), Metro-North's own operations center responsible for train movements and signal/interlocking status. RTC typically hears about a fault like this one of three ways: a train engineer or conductor radios it in during their run, a signal maintainer either monitoring the interlocking remotely or dispatched to investigate reports it, or the interlocking's own diagnostic system throws an automated fault alert that RTC sees on their board. RTC then hands the report down to whoever owns rider communications and case tracking, which is the role the "dispatcher" persona represents in this demo. The dispatcher (and the Agentforce agent standing in for them) isn't diagnosing the signal fault; they're receiving RTC's report and turning it into a structured record and a public message, fast. It's worth naming that distinction out loud with an MTA audience; they'll know the RTC/dispatch split already and will notice if the demo glosses over it.
